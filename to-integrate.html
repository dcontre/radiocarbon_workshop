Using Radiocarbon Dating in Archaeology

8 to integrate

[integrate exercises with OxCal]

[example of papers applying Bayesian/14C in Andes Centrales]

What are we measuring? Age. Age of what? The sample that we’ve collected from a context of interest [e.g., peanut shell]. More specifically, we’re measuring the age of the carbon in that shell, which is to say time at which it stopped incorporating (atmospheric) carbon. The age of this sample provides us with a dated event.  What is the age we want to know? Construction (or deposition, or death, or whatever) – this is our target event. How dated event relates to target event is perhaps the most important question that we can answer.

HCV Lambayeque cemetery as example. Temporal distributions (1 burial/year for 300 years? Normal distribution centered on middle of use? Sigma distribution?). Different scenarios of use…imply different likelihoods for what we might sample. If the goal is to determine when cemetery use began/peaked/ended, the nature of our sample makes a huge difference. As does the uncertainty of dating!

Lambayeque cemetery example – typological dating tells us something, but pretty imprecisely, and assumes synchrony with other sites/valleys, when questioning synchrony might be exactly what we want to do. Limits of typological dating even more salient in regions/times when typological dating tells us less. Radiocarbon dating as a solution? Yes, but…challenges for radiocarbon at both level of dating individual interment, and at level of assessing cemetery use. Abundance of dateable material, all with challenges for matching dated event to target event. Introduce old wood, heirlooms / residence time, and marine reservoir. No magic bullet…make arguments about which samples are most likely to match age of target event. And model results…because (Bayesian logic) we know more about the samples than just their radiocarbon age, and it would be silly not to incorporate that knowledge into our age estimates. Bayesian modeling is basically a tool for doing that formally. Need images of a) plan view of cemetery overall (distribution of interments), and b) an individual burial (illustration of dateable material).

Simulated cemetery data (distribution of dated interments) —> simulated cemetery dates (one version simple (dated events assumed to match target events), then one version more complicated (marine diet, heirlooms, etc))

Is radiocarbon the only tool we have for measuring age? Far from it. Why focus on radiocarbon, then? Relatively cheap and simple; complications well understood and manageable(ish); most importantly provides calendar ages.

  1. Measuring age: accuracy and precision
    1. Accepting uncertainty
      1. archaeologists do this all the time, in dating by period
        1. in doing so, we rely on classification, established chronological frameworks and transitive reasoning (this object is Chimu; Chimu objects were produced, used and discarded between approx 1200 – 1550 CE; therefore this object was produced, used, and discarded (or maybe only one of those – depends on context in which object is found and on what we want to know) between approx 1200 – 1550 CE.
      2. duration vs imprecision
        1. If we describe an object as “Cupisnique”, and – consulting various proposals for when Cupisnique-style materials were produced, used, and discarded – thereby determine that that something is therefore between 3400 and 2500 years old (that is, dates to approx 1500 – 500 BCE), what do we mean?
          1. We don’t mean that that object was in use for 1000 years! Rather, it was produced, used, and discarded at some point in that span.
      3. Why do we want higher precision than artifact classification generally gives (excepting cases like the U.S. Southwest and the European Bronze/Iron Ages, where typologies can have resolutions of decades)? 1. Interpretive goals of archaeology (what do we want to know?)
        1. Sequence (where direct stratigraphic relationships are unavailable)
        2. Synchrony (with other archaeological evidence)
        3. Juxtaposition with other data (especially paleoclimate and paleoenvironment)
  2. Target event vs. Dated event
    1. What do we want to know, and how to we try to figure it out?
    2. Radiocarbon is not the only tool, but in most cases it’s the highest precision likely to be available.

    2. Management of uncertainty through integration of radiocarbon dates and other chronological information

    1. Imprecision + calibration create substantial uncertainy in our age measurements
    2. Even worse, it’s not symmetrically distributed
